Learn to read research

New to scientific papers? These short guides help you read faster, judge what you find, and use the right words.

Read a paper in three passes

  1. Skim (5 minutes). Read the title, abstract, headings and conclusion. Decide if it’s worth your time.
  2. Understand. Study the figures and the methods. Skip the fine detail.
  3. Challenge. Ask what could be wrong and what would change the authors’ mind.

Can you trust it?

  1. Does it have a DOI and named authors?
  2. Is it peer reviewed, or a preprint?
  3. How many people or animals were studied?
  4. Has it been retracted or corrected?
  5. Does it show a link, or prove a cause?

Cite it properly

Open any paper on NeuroLab and copy its APA, BibTeX or RIS citation. Always cite the original study, not a news story about it, and keep the DOI so others can find it.

Neuroscience glossary

Neuron

A nerve cell that sends electrical and chemical signals.

Synapse

The junction where one neuron passes a signal to another.

Action potential

A brief electrical spike that travels along a neuron’s axon.

Neurotransmitter

A chemical messenger released at synapses, such as dopamine, serotonin or glutamate.

Neuroplasticity

The brain’s ability to change its connections with experience.

Glia

Support cells in the nervous system, including astrocytes, microglia and oligodendrocytes.

Hippocampus

A brain region important for forming new memories and finding your way.

Cortex

The folded outer layer of the brain, involved in perception, thought and movement.

EEG

Electroencephalography: records the brain’s electrical activity from the scalp.

fMRI

Functional MRI: tracks blood-oxygen changes as an indirect sign of brain activity.

Optogenetics

Using light to switch genetically modified neurons on or off.

Connectome

A map of the connections between neurons.

Blood-brain barrier

A tight layer of cells that controls what passes from the blood into the brain.

Neurodegeneration

The gradual loss of neurons, as in Alzheimer’s or Parkinson’s disease.

Peer review

Expert checking of a paper before it is published.

Preprint

A paper shared publicly before peer review.

How submissions work

You upload your manuscript through the submit form. An editor checks scope and research ethics. Accepted work is shared as a preprint, clearly labelled as not yet peer reviewed. Volunteer reviewers then comment openly, and authors can revise.

Become a reviewer

Reviewers give structured, constructive feedback in their area of expertise. Include your ORCID iD so authors can see who you are.
